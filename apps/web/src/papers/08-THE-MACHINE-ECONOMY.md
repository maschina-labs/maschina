## 1. The premise

The payment rails for software arrived before the safety did. The x402 standard carries the support
of Visa, Stripe, Google, AWS, Coinbase, Circle, Mastercard and the Solana Foundation through a
foundation formed in April 2026 [8], and Solana settles roughly half to two thirds of that traffic [7].
Honest analysis of the same traffic suggests only a small fraction of its value is genuinely originated
by software at the time of writing [7].

That gap between the rails existing and the rails being used is exactly the gap this document is about.
Software will not be handed meaningful money until handing it money is safe, and once it is, the
activity that follows is not software buying things from people. It is machines transacting with each
other.

## 2. Delegation

A machine may hand a portion of its budget to another machine, under limits always tighter than its own.
Never looser. The delegation is recorded on both sides, and the delegating machine remains accountable
for the whole.

![Figure 1. Budgets narrow as they are delegated. This is the only direction the system permits.](figures/fig-08-delegation.png)

This is what turns a collection of machines into a structure. One machine allocates capital according to
conditions. Others execute. The record shows exactly which decision belonged to which, which is
something no human managed portfolio has ever been able to produce.

## 3. Payment

Machines pay for what they use: data, signals, compute, bandwidth, and the work of other machines.
Payments are ordinary actions subject to the same mandate and caps, recorded like everything else.

A machine that sells something is a business with a wallet, a cost base and a record, operated by
whoever built it.

## 4. Work and schedules

A machine can hold a schedule: working hours, active periods, quiet periods, and a rhythm of paying out
to its owner. When someone says they want something working while they sleep, they mean it literally,
and the system should express it literally.

## 5. Competition

Machines compete in the open. The same job attempted by many machines, ranked by outcomes taken from
records rather than claims, over identical periods, net of fees, against the benchmark of having done
nothing.

Competition in simulation costs nothing and risks nothing, which makes it the natural front door: equal
starting capital, live prices, real decisions, and a leaderboard that is a fair comparison because
everybody began from the same place. Competition with real capital is ranked in divisions by size,
because a ranking dominated by whoever brought the most money measures nothing.

## 6. Publishing and copying

A machine that works can be published. Others copy it, running their own instance with their own funds,
their own wallet and their own limits. The creator earns for as long as the copies run.

Two design choices carry most of the weight.

**Copies are bounded by the copier.** A published machine cannot carry its author's risk appetite into
somebody else's account.

**Capacity is finite and priced.** A strategy degrades as capital crowds into it, which is a fact about
markets rather than a platform rule. Copy slots are limited and auctioned, and the creator chooses
between few expensive copies and many cheap ones. That is price discovery on capability, and it makes
the marketplace a market rather than a catalogue.

## 7. Backing

Publishing requires a stake behind the machine. If copies fail in ways the original's record never
showed, copiers are compensated from it.

The amount backing a published machine is displayed beside it. A claim supported by the claimant's own
money at risk is the only trust signal in this industry worth displaying.

## 8. Insurance

A machine may underwrite another machine's drawdown for a premium, priced from the record.

This is possible only because behaviour is provable. An insurer that can read a machine's complete
history, including its worst week, is pricing evidence rather than a story.

## 9. Credit

Every machine accumulates a signed, verifiable history of what it did, what it earned, what it lost, and
how it behaved under stress. That is a credit file.

Once credit files exist, capital can be allocated to machines on evidence. A machine with a long record
of disciplined behaviour can be extended credit priced by that record, secured by the same custody rules
that govern everything else, with funds held under the same wallet policy as everything else.

This is the furthest reaching consequence of an append-only record, and it is unavailable to any system
whose history can be edited by the party being assessed.

## 10. Capital markets

The natural end of the same line: people funding machines they do not own, creators earning a share of
what those machines produce, and custody designed so that funds return to whoever provided them.

This is asset management with the custody problem solved structurally rather than contractually. It also
carries the heaviest regulatory weight of anything in these papers, and it arrives with a jurisdiction
and a structure or it does not arrive.

## 11. What does not happen here

Every mechanism above is adversarial against markets or against the platform. None is adversarial
against a user who did not choose the contest.

A system where the clever move is taking money from a less sophisticated participant produces one
excellent quarter and then an empty market. The mechanisms here are designed so that the profitable
behaviour and the honest behaviour are the same behaviour, which is the only arrangement that survives
contact with people who are cleverer than its designers.
